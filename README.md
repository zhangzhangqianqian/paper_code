# Lightweight State-Conditioned Dynamic Task Sharing for Multi-Energy Load Forecasting: Explicit Evaluation of Negative Transfer

## 1. Introduction

With the coordinated operation of electricity, cooling, heating, gas, and other energy carriers in integrated energy systems (IESs), accurate multi-energy load forecasting has become essential for energy planning, optimal dispatch, and demand response. A recent review systematically summarizes the applications and development trends of artificial-intelligence methods for multi-energy load forecasting in IESs [1]. For small-sample IESs, neural networks, Gaussian processes, and multi-task learning have been combined to exploit complementary information across multiple load types [2]. For user-level IESs, global–local attention and multiple decoders have been used to represent shared patterns and task-specific dynamics separately [3]. Unlike conventional electricity load forecasting, multi-energy load forecasting must capture both the temporal patterns of individual loads and the cross-energy relationships induced by energy conversion devices, weather conditions, calendar attributes, and user behavior. Training an independent model for each load may discard useful information shared across tasks, whereas indiscriminately feeding all variables into a common model may obscure load-specific dynamics. Joint multi-energy forecasting must therefore balance the exploitation of common patterns against the preservation of task-specific characteristics.

This balance is difficult because the relationships among energy tasks are state-dependent and potentially directional. A dynamic-coupling study characterizes time-varying relationships among different energy loads [4]. Research that explicitly considers meteorological variations further shows that weather conditions can change the association patterns among load types [5]. Cooling and electricity loads may exhibit stronger synchronous variation during hot seasons, whereas the dominant factors affecting heating demand may change during cold seasons. Calendar attributes such as weekday/weekend status and abrupt meteorological changes can also reshape cross-load relationships. Moreover, the predictive value of information transferred between two energy loads is not necessarily symmetric. For example, the historical electricity load may help forecast the cooling load, whereas the historical cooling load may not benefit electricity-load forecasting to the same extent. The key challenge is therefore not merely to increase the number of input variables, but to determine, for each target task under the current operating state, whether information should be shared, which source task should provide it, and how strongly it should affect the prediction.

Multi-task learning and spatiotemporal attention have recently been used to extract intra-task temporal dependence and inter-task coupling simultaneously; hierarchical multi-task learning is a representative example [6]. Graph-attention multi-task models further aggregate information according to structural relationships among tasks or energy nodes [7]. In parallel, multivariate time-series research has investigated how relationships among variables can be used selectively without discarding the temporal patterns specific to each variable. iTransformer represents the complete history of each variable independently and then learns relationships among these variable representations [8]. LIFT identifies leading variables that anticipate future changes in a target variable and introduces only cross-variable information with predictive value [9]. SOFTS first aggregates information from multiple variables into a global core representation and then redistributes it to individual variables, enabling cross-variable interaction at a relatively low computational cost [10]. These studies demonstrate the predictive value of cross-task information while indicating that neither complete independence nor unrestricted mixing is universally optimal across datasets and operating states.

Recent energy-forecasting studies have begun to address these issues. Dynamic-coupling research analyzes cross-load relationships across long-term trends, periodic variations, and short-term fluctuations, providing motivation for state-conditioned information transfer [4]. Multi-task balancing research uses sharing matrices and gradient coordination to handle optimization conflicts among energy tasks [11]. A frequency-enhanced multi-task mixture-of-experts network for small-sample settings further makes negative-transfer mitigation an explicit design objective [12]. Unlike existing approaches that rely on frequency decomposition and multi-expert gating, this study does not introduce frequency decomposition or a mixture-of-experts network. Instead, it uses lightweight temporal encoders with controlled parameter counts and state-conditioned directional task gates for selective information transfer, and directly evaluates the relative transfer gain for each load task against an architecture-matched single-task model. The objective is therefore not to propose another generic dynamic relationship model or another negative-transfer mitigation mechanism, but to investigate three connected questions within a unified framework. First, can cross-task sharing intensity and source selection vary with the load, weather, and calendar state of each sample and with the forecast horizon while assigning different weights to opposite transfer directions? Second, can improvements in the average error of the joint model conceal performance degradation for individual tasks, seasons, or forecast horizons? Third, can the benefits of dynamic relationship modeling be achieved under controlled parameter and computational budgets? Research on multi-task resource allocation shows that tasks may receive unequal resources and gains from joint optimization [13]. Multi-task linear forecasting also highlights the importance of unequal benefits across tasks in collaborative modeling [14]. Negative transfer occurs when a joint model produces a larger error for a task than an architecture-matched single-task model. Reporting only the average error across tasks is therefore insufficient to determine whether every load task actually benefits from information sharing.

To investigate these questions, this study develops a lightweight state-conditioned dynamic task-sharing framework. Depthwise-separable temporal convolutional encoders whose receptive fields cover the complete 24-hour input window first extract separate temporal representations for electricity, cooling, heating, and gas tasks. Weather, calendar, and load-state information is then combined with forecast-horizon embeddings. A two-stage directed task router separately estimates the overall sharing intensity of each target task and the relative allocation across source tasks, thereby decomposing the decision into whether to share, from which task, and to what extent. Source representations are transformed through low-rank directed projections and injected into the target representation through message normalization and controlled residual fusion. When the sharing intensity is zero, the target task retains a strict task-independent prediction path. Task–horizon-specific heads subsequently produce forecasts for the four loads over the next one to four hours. The experiments compare architecture-matched single-task models, fixed-sharing multi-task models, the original dynamic-gating model, and the proposed model. Forecast errors and relative transfer gains are evaluated across tasks, seasons, and forecast horizons. Parameter count, theoretical computational complexity, and training and inference time on the same hardware platform are also reported to determine whether the benefits of dynamic sharing justify their computational cost.

The empirical study uses the publicly available Kitakyushu Energy Station Data, which contain hourly energy supply, consumption, and weather records from an energy station and campus buildings in Kitakyushu Science and Research Park, Japan, and are archived on Figshare [15]. The formal experiments use data from 2015 to 2021 and define electricity demand, cooling demand, heating demand, and energy-station-side natural-gas consumption as four forecasting tasks. The gas task aggregates natural-gas use by energy-station equipment, including the gas engine, fuel cell, absorption chillers, and gas boiler; it is not interpreted as an independently metered end-user gas load for a single building. The data descriptor reports an earthquake-related gap in March 2011 [15], and the local audit identified anomalous load fields in the 2014 files; neither period is included in the formal experimental range. MMoE-lite is used only as a lightweight external baseline that retains the expert-routing idea. It excludes the Frequency and STIM modules and is not presented as a reproduction of the complete Frequency-STIM-MMoE model proposed by Shao et al.

The main contributions of this study are as follows:

1. Multi-energy joint forecasting is formulated as a state-conditioned directional task-sharing problem. By comparing the joint model with structurally matched single-task models for each load and calculating relative transfer gains, the evaluation directly determines whether joint training degrades any task and whether the dynamic gate alleviates such degradation.
2. A shared–task-specific framework is developed by combining full-window lightweight temporal encoders, state and forecast-horizon representations, a two-stage directed router for sharing intensity and source allocation, low-rank message projections, and task–horizon-specific forecasting heads. Cross-energy transfer can therefore vary with the current state and forecast distance, while a strict task-independent path is retained when sharing is switched off.
3. An evaluation protocol covering average accuracy, task-level negative transfer, stability across seasons and forecast horizons, and computational cost is established to distinguish overall joint-learning gains from individual task benefits and complexity overhead.
