# Lightweight State-Conditioned Dynamic Task Sharing for Multi-Energy Load Forecasting with Explicit Negative Transfer Evaluation

## 1. Introduction

With the coordinated operation of electricity, cooling, heating, gas, and other energy carriers in integrated energy systems (IESs), accurate multi-energy load forecasting has become essential for energy planning, optimal dispatch, and demand response [1–3]. Unlike conventional electricity load forecasting, multi-energy load forecasting must capture both the temporal patterns of individual loads and the cross-energy relationships induced by energy conversion devices, weather conditions, calendar attributes, and user behavior [2,3]. Training an independent model for each load may discard useful information shared across tasks, whereas indiscriminately feeding all variables into a common model may obscure load-specific dynamics. Joint multi-energy forecasting must therefore balance the exploitation of common patterns against the preservation of task-specific characteristics.

This balance is difficult because the relationships among energy tasks are state dependent and potentially directional. Cooling and electricity loads may exhibit stronger synchronous variation during hot seasons, whereas the dominant factors affecting heating demand may change during cold seasons. Calendar attributes such as weekday/weekend status and abrupt meteorological changes can also reshape cross-load relationships [4,5]. Moreover, the predictive value of information transferred between two energy loads is not necessarily symmetric. For example, the historical state of electricity load may help forecast cooling load, whereas the historical state of cooling load may not benefit electricity-load forecasting to the same extent. The key challenge is therefore not merely to increase the number of input variables, but to determine, for each target task under the current operating state, whether information should be shared, from which task it should be obtained, and how strongly it should affect the prediction.

Multi-task learning, spatiotemporal attention, graph attention, and shared-encoder–multiple-decoder architectures have recently been applied to multi-energy load forecasting to model intra-task temporal dependence and inter-task coupling simultaneously [2,3,6,7]. In parallel, multivariate time-series research has investigated how relationships among variables can be used selectively without discarding the temporal patterns specific to each variable. iTransformer represents the complete history of each variable independently and then learns relationships among these variable representations. LIFT identifies leading variables that anticipate future changes in a target variable and introduces only cross-variable information with predictive value. SOFTS first aggregates information from multiple variables into a global core representation and then redistributes it to individual variables, enabling cross-variable interaction at a relatively low computational cost [8–10]. These studies demonstrate the predictive value of cross-task information while indicating that neither complete independence nor unrestricted mixing is universally optimal across datasets and operating states.

Recent energy-forecasting studies have begun to address these issues. Existing work has characterized time-varying cross-load coupling by examining the dynamic relationships among different energy loads across long-term trends, periodic variations, and short-term fluctuations [4]; multi-level task-sharing matrices and gradient balancing have been introduced to coordinate multi-energy tasks [11]; and a frequency-enhanced multi-task mixture-of-experts network for small-sample settings has explicitly targeted negative-transfer mitigation [12]. Unlike existing approaches that rely on frequency decomposition and multi-expert gating, this study does not introduce frequency decomposition or a mixture-of-experts network. Instead, it uses parameter-controlled temporal encoders and state-conditioned directional task gates for selective information transfer, and directly evaluates the transfer gain of each energy task against an architecture-matched single-task model. The objective is therefore not to reintroduce dynamic relationship modeling or negative-transfer mitigation, but to investigate three connected questions within a unified framework. First, can cross-task sharing strengths vary with the load, weather, and calendar state of each sample while assigning different weights to different transfer directions? Second, can improvements in average joint error conceal performance degradation for individual tasks, seasons, or forecast horizons? Third, can the benefits of dynamic relationship modeling be achieved under controlled parameter and computational budgets? General multi-task learning and multivariate forecasting studies also indicate that tasks may receive unequal resources and gains from joint optimization [13,14]. Negative transfer occurs when a joint model produces a larger error for a task than an architecture-matched single-task model. Reporting only the average error across tasks is therefore insufficient to determine whether every energy task actually benefits from information sharing.

To investigate these questions, this study develops a lightweight state-conditioned dynamic task-sharing framework. Parameter-controlled temporal encoders first extract short-term variations and periodic patterns from electricity, cooling, heating, and other load series. The task representations are then combined with weather and calendar variables to calculate a sample-dependent information-transfer weight for each source–target task pair. Each weight represents how strongly information should be transferred from a source task to a target task under the current operating state. Gated cross-task information is fused with the target representation through a residual connection, after which task-specific forecasting heads produce multi-step forecasts for individual loads. The framework does not assume that all tasks are always mutually beneficial; instead, it learns state-dependent decisions about whether to share information, from which task, and to what extent. The experiments compare architecture-matched single-task models, fixed-sharing multi-task models, and the proposed dynamic-sharing model. Forecast errors and relative transfer gains are evaluated by task, season, and forecast horizon. Parameter count, theoretical operation count, and training and inference time under a unified hardware environment are also reported to determine whether the benefits of dynamic sharing justify their computational cost.

The main contributions of this study are as follows:

1. Multi-energy joint forecasting is formulated as a state-conditioned directional task-sharing problem. By comparing the joint model with structurally matched single-task models for each load and calculating relative transfer gains, the evaluation directly determines whether joint training degrades any task and whether the dynamic gate alleviates such degradation.
2. A shared–private framework comprising lightweight temporal encoders, a state encoder, a directed dynamic task-relation gate, and task-specific forecasting heads is developed. It allows cross-energy information transfer to vary with the current load and external conditions while preserving load-specific dynamics.
3. An evaluation protocol covering average accuracy, task-level negative transfer, stability across seasons and forecast horizons, and computational cost is established to distinguish overall joint-learning gains from individual task benefits and complexity overhead.
